Introduction

The Internet is a worldwide network of interconnected computer networks that enable devices anywhere in the world to communicate with each other. It did not start out as a consumer product but as a U.S. government research effort in the late 1950s and 1960s to build a communication system that could withstand disruption. It eventually became ARPANET, a small experimental network that connected a few universities and research computers. This experiment evolved over the next few decades into the decentralized, world-wide infrastructure we know today as the Internet. Key technologies such as packet switching and TCP/IP were developed. The timeline below shows the major milestones in that evolution, from its military-research origins to the protocols that still form the basis of the Internet today. The timeline now runs from those origins all the way to the present day, covering the arrival of commercial access, Wi-Fi, IPv6, broadband, and mobile 4G and 5G networks, followed by the three key technologies behind it: packet switching, TCP/IP and DNS.

Timeline of the Internet

1958: ARPA is created

The U.S. created ARPA in response to the Soviet launch of Sputnik, establishing the government research body that would eventually fund the network that became the Internet.

Source: historyofinformation.com

1961: Kleinrock publishes packet-switching paper

Leonard Kleinrock publishes "Information Flow in Large Communication Nets” Introduced packet-switching theory, the foundational idea of breaking data into pieces for transmission, which still underlies how data moves across networks today.

Source: lk.cs.ucla.edu

1969: First ARPANET message (UCLA-SRI)

ARPANET's first host-to-host message is sent (UCLA to Stanford Research Institute): The first real-world test of a working computer network; considered the technical starting point of the Internet.

Source: internetsociety.org

1972(disputed): Tomlinson's networked email

Ray Tomlinson develops networked email at BBN, choosing “@” to separate user from host — The first major practical, person-to-person use of the network, and the ancestor of every email address format still used today. Note: Sources disagree on the exact year. The Internet Society's own history document lists 1972, while Ray Tomlinson stated in later interviews that 1971 is the correct year and that 1972 was a mistaken date that became widely repeated.

Source: internetsociety.org

1973: Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn begin developing TCP/IP

Created the shared protocol that let separate, incompatible computer networks communicate with each other, forming the technical backbone of the modern Internet.

Source: internetsociety.org

1983: ARPANET fully switches to TCP/IP ("Flag Day")

January 1 1983, Widely regarded as the technical "birthday" of the Internet as a unified network.

Source: internetsociety.org

1990: ARPANET officially decommissioned

Marked the end of the original experimental network, which by then had been fully succeeded by NSFNET and the broader Internet.

Source: internetsociety.org

1995: NSFNET is retired and the Internet is commercialised

In April 1995 the NSF stopped funding the NSFNET backbone, and commercial providers took over the main network links. This opened the Internet to businesses and the general public.

Source: internetsociety.org

1997: IEEE 802.11 wireless LAN standard (Wi-Fi)

The IEEE approved the 802.11 standard on June 26, 1997, defining wireless local area networking. The Wi-Fi Alliance was formed in 1999 and adopted the name "Wi-Fi" in 2000. Devices could now join a network without cables.

Source: standards.ieee.org

1998: IPv6 is specified (RFC 2460)

IPv6 was published as a standard in December 1998 to solve the shortage of IPv4 addresses. Its permanent, large-scale deployment began with World IPv6 Launch on June 6, 2012.

Source: rfc-editor.org

2000s: Broadband replaces dial-up

According to Pew Research, about 1% of U.S. adults had home broadband in 2000, rising to roughly 60-64% by 2010. Always-on, faster connections made video, streaming and modern web applications practical.

Source: pewresearch.org

2007: The iPhone brings the mobile Internet to the mainstream

Apple announced the iPhone on January 9, 2007, describing it as a phone, an iPod and an Internet communications device in one. Browsing the full web from a pocket device became an everyday activity.

Source: apple.com

2010: 4G (IMT-Advanced) is confirmed by the ITU

In late 2010 the ITU recognised LTE-Advanced and WirelessMAN-Advanced as IMT-Advanced ("4G") technologies, targeting up to 1 Gbit/s for low mobility. Mobile data speeds became comparable to home broadband.

Source: rcrwireless.com

2015-2021: 5G standardisation (IMT-2020)

The ITU's IMT-2020 framework (Recommendation M.2083, 2015; M.2150, February 2021) defined 5G, with higher speeds, lower latency and support for very large numbers of connected devices.

Source: itu.int

Key Technologies

Packet Switching

The method of breaking data into small, independently-routed packets rather than sending it as one continuous stream, allowing a decentralized and resilient network to function.

Source: lk.cs.ucla.edu

TCP/IP

The common set of rules that lets different networks and devices, regardless of hardware or software, communicate with each other reliably; this is the technology that unifies the Internet as a single "network of networks".

Source: internetsociety.org

DNS

Invented by Paul Mockapetris in November 1983, DNS replaced a single manually updated file (HOSTS.TXT) with a distributed, scalable naming system, allowing users to type human-readable names instead of numeric IP addresses.

Source: internetsociety.org
